Turn Part of Your Home Equity Into Cash

Start With Your Home Equity

Everything starts with understanding your home equity. It is generally the difference between your home's estimated value and your current mortgage balance.

Estimated Home Equity = Estimated Home Value − Current Mortgage Balance

Example: estimated home value $450,000 − mortgage balance $275,000 = estimated equity $175,000.

Important: $175,000 of estimated equity does not necessarily mean you can receive $175,000 in cash. Actual borrowing capacity depends on factors including lender loan-to-value limits, credit, income, the property, your mortgage balance, closing costs and underwriting requirements.

How Does a Cash-Out Refinance Work?

A cash-out refinance replaces your existing first mortgage. You take out a new, larger mortgage, the old mortgage is paid off, and the difference may become available to you as cash after applicable costs and adjustments.

This is the key difference from a HELOC: a cash-out refinance generally replaces your existing first mortgage. It does not simply add a separate cash account while leaving the original mortgage unchanged.

What Can Cash-Out Refinance Funds Be Used For?

Homeowners investigate cash-out refinancing for many reasons. Common examples include:

  • consolidating debts
  • paying higher-interest credit-card balances
  • paying off other loans
  • home improvements
  • major planned expenses
  • education expenses
  • other financial goals

Important trade-off: using home equity this way converts part of your equity into mortgage debt secured by your property. It is not automatically financially beneficial.

Using Home Equity to Consolidate Debt

A homeowner may be carrying several balances at once — a mortgage, credit cards, personal loans and other debts. A cash-out refinance may provide a lump sum that can potentially be used to pay some of those balances. This is often called cash out refinance for debt consolidation.

Why people consider it: some unsecured debts, particularly credit cards, may carry higher interest rates than mortgage financing. However, refinancing does not automatically save money.

Important considerations if you are weighing a cash out refinance to pay off debt or a cash out refinance to pay credit cards:

  • your current mortgage rate versus a new mortgage rate
  • closing costs
  • the loan term and amount borrowed
  • total interest over time
  • converting unsecured debt into debt secured by your home
  • whether the credit-card balances actually stay paid off afterward

Keep in mind that debt consolidation restructures debt — it does not eliminate it.

Cash-Out Refinance vs HELOC

These two options are structured quite differently. Here is the cash out refinance vs HELOC comparison at a glance:

Cash-Out Refinance

  • generally replaces the existing mortgage
  • creates a new mortgage balance
  • may provide proceeds as a lump sum after closing
  • you receive the cash rather than drawing from a line
  • mortgage terms and rate may change

HELOC

  • normally exists alongside the existing first mortgage
  • provides a revolving credit line
  • funds may be drawn as needed during the draw period
  • commonly has variable-rate characteristics
  • payments can change

There is no universally better option — the appropriate structure depends on your situation, your existing mortgage rate, how much you need and how you expect to use it.

What Happens to My Existing Mortgage?

A cash-out refinance is a refinance. Your existing mortgage is generally paid off and replaced with the new mortgage. That means it is worth considering more than just how much cash you can receive. Compare:

  • your existing mortgage rate versus a potential new rate
  • your remaining mortgage term versus a new loan term
  • your monthly payment
  • closing costs
  • total borrowing cost
  • break-even considerations

This matters especially if you already have a favorable existing mortgage rate.

Costs and Considerations

Cash-out refinancing may involve costs. Potential considerations include:

  • closing costs
  • appraisal / valuation requirements where applicable
  • lender fees
  • interest rate
  • new loan amount
  • mortgage term
  • monthly payment
  • total interest
  • available equity and loan-to-value requirements
  • credit / income underwriting

Specific fees and thresholds vary by lender; we do not publish lender-specific figures.

Is Cash-Out Refinancing Right for Every Homeowner?

No — it is not automatically the right solution simply because equity exists. Relevant considerations include:

  • the reason for accessing cash
  • the amount needed
  • your existing mortgage rate versus a new rate
  • how long you expect to stay in the home
  • closing costs
  • your ability to repay
  • alternatives such as a HELOC or a home equity loan

This is educational information; it is not individualized financial advice and does not guarantee suitability.

Common cash-out refinance questions

How does a cash out refinance work, in one sentence?
It replaces your existing mortgage with a new, larger mortgage and may provide the difference as cash after applicable costs and payoff amounts.
Cash out refinance vs HELOC — which is better?
Neither is universally better. A cash-out refinance replaces your mortgage and gives a lump sum; a HELOC sits alongside your mortgage and gives a revolving line you draw from as needed.
Can I use a cash out refinance to pay off credit cards?
Potentially, yes — proceeds may be used to pay balances. Be aware this converts unsecured debt into debt secured by your home, and it restructures rather than eliminates debt.
How much equity do I have?
Estimate it as your home value minus your mortgage balance, then confirm your situation with the Home Equity Calculator.
How to get equity out of my house?
Common routes are a cash-out refinance, a HELOC, or a home equity loan. Which fits depends on your goal and your existing mortgage rate.
