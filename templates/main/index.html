Your Home Equity Options

All three options use the value you have already built in your home. They differ in how you receive the money, how you repay it, and how your existing mortgage is affected.

Cash-Out Refinance

You replace your existing mortgage with a larger one and receive part of the difference in cash. Often chosen when someone wants one new loan, one payment, and usually a fixed rate.

Cash-out refinance explained →

HELOC

A Home Equity Line of Credit is a revolving line secured by your equity. You can borrow during a draw period and typically repay only interest at first, then principal plus interest afterwards.

How a HELOC works →

Home Equity Loan

A second loan that gives you a lump sum secured by your home equity, repaid on a fixed schedule — usually alongside your existing mortgage rather than replacing it.

Not sure which one?

That is the most common question we see. Cash-out refinance, a HELOC and a home equity loan can all “get cash out of your house” — but the right choice depends on your rate, your balance, how much equity you have, your goal, and how long you plan to stay.

Understanding Your Options

Clear explanations of the questions homeowners actually ask — not a sales pitch.

Cash-out refinance vs HELOC vs home equity loan

The three options overlap, which is exactly why they are confusing. A cash-out refinance replaces your current mortgage and folds the extra borrowing into one new loan. A HELOC leaves your first mortgage alone and adds a flexible line you draw from as needed. A home equity loan also leaves the first mortgage alone but gives a single lump sum with a fixed repayment schedule. The right answer usually comes down to whether you want to change your existing mortgage, how much you need, and whether you value flexibility over predictability.

How does a HELOC work?

A HELOC is a revolving credit line secured by your equity, similar in spirit to a credit card but tied to your home. It usually has two phases. During the draw period you can borrow, repay, and borrow again, and many HELOCs allow interest-only payments at this stage. When the draw period ends, the line enters repayment: you can no longer draw, and your payment rises because you are now paying down principal as well as interest. That jump — the payment after the draw period — surprises a lot of homeowners, so it is worth modelling before you sign. Rate changes matter too, because most HELOCs have variable rates.

How much can you borrow, and do you qualify?

Lenders generally look at how much equity you have, your credit profile, your income and your existing debts. Common HELOC requirements include a minimum amount of equity, a workable debt-to-income ratio, and acceptable credit. If your credit is imperfect, a HELOC with bad credit or a home equity loan with bad credit is sometimes still possible, but expect stricter terms — and be cautious with any offer that promises “guaranteed approval” or “no credit check”. The honest path is to understand your realistic range before you apply.

How do I get equity out of my house?

Broadly, there are three routes: refinance (cash-out), open a line (HELOC), or take a second loan (home equity loan). Which one fits depends on your goal. Refinancing to a lower rate while taking cash can make sense; a HELOC suits ongoing or staged needs such as renovations; a home equity loan suits a one-time, known expense. Comparing them on your own figures is the fastest way to see which fits.

Frequently asked questions

Which is cheaper, a HELOC or a cash-out refinance?
It depends on your current mortgage rate, how much you need, and how long you will keep the loan. If your current rate is already low, adding a HELOC may preserve it; if refinancing lowers your rate, a cash-out refinance can do double duty. Run both against your numbers.
Can I get equity out of my house without refinancing?
Yes. A HELOC or a home equity loan both let you access equity without replacing your existing mortgage.
What happens to my HELOC payment after the draw period?
It usually increases, because repayment now includes principal. The size of the increase depends on your balance, term and rate — model it in advance.
Do I need perfect credit?
No, but better credit generally means better terms. If your credit is damaged, focus on understanding your realistic options rather than responding to guaranteed-approval offers.
